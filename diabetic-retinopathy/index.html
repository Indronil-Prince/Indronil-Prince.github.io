01 / OVERVIEW

Classifying the severity of diabetic retinopathy

Computer vision and handcrafted retinal features for five-class disease-severity classification.

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) can cause progressive damage to retinal blood vessels. This study investigates a feature-based machine learning pipeline for identifying five levels of disease severity using color fundus photographs.

Rather than training a deep classifier end-to-end, the approach extracts interpretable retinal morphology signals from exudates, blood vessels and microaneurysms. These measurements are fed into Random Forest, Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Naive Bayes classifiers.

Parallel feature-extraction branches
ExudatesOptic-disc removal, dilation, median filtering and intensity thresholding.
Blood vesselsMulti-scale morphological filtering, subtraction, area filtering and binarization.
MicroaneurysmsGreen channel, CLAHE, morphology and contour-area extraction.

Each of the three branches contributes two reported descriptor types: a histogram and a zeroth Hu moment. The resulting six feature families are scaled and supplied to conventional classifiers.

Dataset, image processing and evaluation

Size normalizationInput 1024 × 1024 → 350 × 350
EnhancementGreen-channel extraction, CLAHE
DescriptorsHistograms and zeroth Hu moments for exudates, vessels and microaneurysms
Feature scalingStandardScaler
AlgorithmsRandom Forest, Support Vector Machine, Naive Bayes
EvaluationAccuracy, macro-averaged sensitivity and macro-averaged specificity

The five labels are healthy, mild NPDR, moderate NPDR, severe NPDR and proliferative DR (PDR). Macro-averaging gives each class equal weight when computing sensitivity and specificity. The reported classifiers are evaluated on the same feature-based formulation.

Clinical scope: this is a retrospective research experiment, not a clinically validated diagnostic tool.

05 / DISCUSSION

Interpretability, limitations and next steps

Why this representation matters

Exudate, vessel and microaneurysm measurements correspond to observable retinal features. Feature-based models can be examined in terms of which disease indicators contribute to the predictions.

Important constraints

The method depends on image-processing quality and manually designed descriptors. Performance may be sensitive to image acquisition and differences in retinal pigmentation, illumination or disease presentation.

A useful future comparison would benchmark this classical vision pipeline against modern CNN or vision-transformer baselines using patient-level splits, external datasets and class-specific performance analysis. The publication itself does not claim such prospective validation.
